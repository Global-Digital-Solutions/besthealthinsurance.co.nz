The Smart Excess Strategy to Cut Your Premiums

Your excess is the most direct lever to lower premiums, but higher excesses only make sense if you have savings to back them up.

How Excess Affects Your Premium

A $250 excess might cost $100/month ($1,200/year). A $500 excess on the same cover costs $85/month ($1,020/year) — a $180 annual saving. A $1,000 excess costs $70/month ($840/year) — a $360 annual saving compared to the lowest excess.

But the higher excess only makes sense if you can afford to pay it out-of-pocket without financial strain.

The Break-Even Math

At what point does a higher excess start saving you money? Simple calculation:

If you claim once every three years on average, a $1,000 excess costs you $1,000 per claim. Your premium is $60/month lower ($720/year). Over three years: you save $2,160 in premiums and spend $1,000 in excess. Net savings: $1,160.

If you claim every year, the $1,000 excess costs $1,000/year, and your premium saving of $720/year means you're actually paying more overall.

If you claim every other year, the $1,000 excess costs $500/year on average, and the premium saving of $720 means you're ahead.

The break-even for a $1,000 excess is if you claim about once every 18 months.

Know Your Claim Pattern

Before choosing a high excess, review your actual healthcare use over the past 3-5 years:

  • ✓How many times did you see a specialist?
  • ✓How many diagnostic tests did you have?
  • ✓How many times would you have claimed if you had insurance?

If you average one claim per year, keep your excess at $250-500. If you average one claim every two years or less, a $1,000 excess makes sense.

The Emergency Fund Requirement

A $1,000 excess only works if you have $1,000 in emergency savings. If your excess would force you to use credit or borrow money, it's too high.

The whole point of high excess is to save on premiums. If paying the excess forces you into debt, you've not actually saved money.

Minimum: ensure you have excess amount × 2 in emergency savings (so you can cover one claim and still have reserves).

The Staged Approach

A practical strategy is increasing your excess as your savings grow:

Ages 25-35, low savings: $250 excess, more expensive premiums. Ages 35-45, building savings: move to $500 excess as you build reserves. Ages 45+, solid savings: move to $1,000 excess, lowest premiums.

This matches your excess to your financial stability rather than forcing an uncomfortable choice.

The Chronic Condition Question

If you have an ongoing condition (arthritis, heart disease, diabetes), you'll claim regularly (specialist consultations every 6-12 months). For these people, a high excess is expensive.

If you claim twice per year at $1,000 excess, you're paying $2,000/year in excess. The $300-400 you save in premiums doesn't offset it.

For chronic conditions, keep your excess moderate ($250-500).

The Hidden Cost: Amount Caps

Some insurers apply annual caps on cover (e.g., "up to $25,000/year in total cover"). Your excess reduces what they pay, not your out-of-pocket cost.

If you need $8,000 of specialist consultations and your excess is $1,000, you pay $1,000 out-of-pocket and the insurer covers $7,000. Your excess applied once, even though you had multiple consultations.

Understanding how excess applies to your specific cover is important. Some insurers apply excess per claim, some per year, some per person. Ask specifically.

The Right Excess for Your Situation

Choose $250 if: - You claim more than once per year on average - You have chronic conditions requiring regular specialist care - You have minimal emergency savings - You prefer predictability

Choose $500 if: - You claim once every 18-24 months on average - You have $500+ emergency savings - You want moderate savings on premiums

Choose $1,000 if: - You claim less than once per year - You have $2,000+ emergency savings - You're comfortable paying $1,000 out-of-pocket if needed

The Premium Trick

Some insurers will quote you a low excess option to show a lower monthly payment, but you must do the annual math. A quote showing $60/month for $250 excess might be $720/year, while $50/month for $1,000 excess is $600/year. Over a decade, the difference compounds to $1,200 saved.

Always calculate annual cost, not monthly cost alone.

Reviewing Your Excess Over Time

Your excess choice should evolve as your life changes. At 30, $1,000 excess might be a stretch. At 50 with solid savings, it makes sense. As you enter later life and claim more frequently, you might reduce it back to $500.

Review your excess choice at each renewal and adjust based on your current financial position and claim pattern.
