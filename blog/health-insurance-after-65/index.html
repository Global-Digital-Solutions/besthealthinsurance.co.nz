Health Insurance After 65: What Changes and What to Watch Out For

Your health insurance at 65 isn't the same as it was at 45. Premiums jump, and insurers apply age-specific limits. Here's what changes.

The Premium Jump at 65

Health insurance premiums increase gradually through your 50s, but around age 65, they often jump significantly. A plan costing $100/month at 60 might be $150+ at 65 — a 50% increase.

This reflects the reality that health insurance claims increase as you age. People over 65 use healthcare 3-4x more frequently than 45-year-olds.

If you're approaching 65, get quotes now for what your insurance will cost at 65. Don't assume your current premium trajectory continues smoothly.

Pre-Existing Condition Reassessment

When you reach 65, insurers sometimes reassess your coverage. If you've been receiving an exclusion on arthritis, your insurer might narrow that exclusion or apply new conditions.

You're not required to switch insurers at 65, but your existing insurer might propose changes at renewal. Read renewal documents carefully.

This is the time to shop around. Switching to a different insurer at 65 might get you better terms than accepting your current insurer's 65-plus adjustments.

Coverage Limits and Age

Some insurers have lifetime limits on cover (e.g., "$50,000 total cover after age 65"). Others have annual caps that don't change with age. Understanding your specific policy is critical.

If you're with an insurer with age-specific limits, understand what happens to your cover at 65, 70, and 75. Some policies become progressively more limited as you age.

Common Conditions at 65+

Arthritis, heart disease, vision changes, hearing loss, diabetes, high blood pressure — these are the common diagnoses at 65+. Most are chronic conditions requiring ongoing specialist management.

Ensure your coverage at 65+ includes major medical access (not just surgical cover). You'll be seeing specialists regularly, and specialist consultation costs need to be covered.

The Choice to Stop Insuring

Some people stop health insurance at 65, either due to cost or belief they won't need it. This is a significant risk.

Even with free public healthcare at 65+, waiting times increase for older people (ironically, when they most need faster access). Private access becomes more valuable, not less.

Maintaining insurance into your 70s and 80s is important unless you can genuinely afford private healthcare out-of-pocket.

Income in Retirement Changes Strategy

If you move from full-time work to superannuation at 65, your income might drop significantly. This affects how you structure insurance:

Higher excess ($1,000) is less suitable if you're on a fixed retirement income. A $500 excess with slightly higher premiums is better for retirees because they can't absorb large unexpected costs.

Switching Insurers at 65

Switching insurers at 65 is harder than at 45. New insurers will reassess your health, and at 65, you have accumulated medical history (past treatments, diagnosed conditions, surgeries). This might result in exclusions that you don't currently have.

If you've been continuously insured from age 40-65, staying with your current insurer might be better than switching and facing new exclusions due to age-related reassessment.

However, if your current insurer's 65-plus terms are bad, switching might still be worth it. Get quotes first.

Critical Illness Cover at 65+

Some insurers stop offering critical illness cover after 65, or it becomes prohibitively expensive. If this coverage is important to you, lock it in before 65.

Similarly, maternity cover (obviously) becomes irrelevant post-55 for most people and should be dropped to save on premiums.

Mental Health Importance at 65+

Retirement transition, loss of work identity, health changes, and sometimes isolation can trigger mental health challenges. Access to counseling and psychology is valuable at 65+.

Ensure your insurance at 65 includes mental health support. Accuro's optional mental health add-on becomes more valuable at this age.

The Age-Specific Conversation with Your Insurer

Contact your insurer at age 64 (before you turn 65) and ask specifically: - How will my premiums change at 65? - Will my coverage change? - Are there any age-specific limits? - What conditions will be reassessed?

Get answers in writing. This gives you time to shop around if you're unhappy with their 65-plus terms.

The Plan: Age 60-65

If you're 60-65, start planning: 1. Review your current coverage at age 64 2. Get quotes from competitors for age 65 3. Decide whether to switch or stay 4. Lock in any add-ons (critical illness, mental health) that might not be available at 65+ 5. Adjust excess and tier based on your retirement income 6. Confirm coverage limits and any age-specific restrictions

Retirement should include secure health insurance, not the assumption of relying on public healthcare. You've worked for decades; investing in good insurance for your 70s and 80s is intelligent.
